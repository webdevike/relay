# The rule

Distilled from the antialgorithm skill by mankidev (https://github.com/mankidev/antialgorithm, MIT, Copyright (c) 2026 mankidev; license kept in `domains/creative/LICENSE-antialgorithm`).

The first answer that comes to mind is the average of everything already made. It feels right because it is familiar, and that is the problem: the person has seen it a thousand times. Notice that pick and move away from it on purpose.

## Where to start

The answer must differ from one caller to the next, and you cannot see what others got. So the start point comes from outside your own taste:

1. The project context is real (a project, a place, a person, a purpose or a constraint): start every option from a fact in it.
2. The context is thin: the request gives one seed per option (axes such as voice, entry, register, or method, color, layout, type, or verb, constraint, scale, pressure, plus a setting, a region and an anchor). Use the seed as the start point. A visual seed also gives a verb and a scale: build the idea behind the piece from them, who does what. You may set aside one axis that truly fights the subject. The region places the setting: names, jokes, habits and the way people speak come from the people of that region, while the words stay in the language of the brief. Then tie the seed to the subject with a reason. A seed is never shown as the answer; it only starts the thinking.

Never pick the start point by thinking about what would be most interesting.

## The default, and moving away

Before each option, find the obvious one: "the default here is X." Do not use X unless the context truly asks for it. That line is the option's `avoided`.

Move away by using what is specific to this case: the project, its world, constraints, audience and odd details. A good choice could only belong here. A default choice could belong anywhere.

Do not swap a cliche for its well-known opposite. "Dark instead of bright" or "minimal instead of busy" is just the second most common answer.

Strange is not the goal. A choice that is odd but has no reason is as empty as a safe one. Each option has a reason you can say in one sentence, and the reason comes from this project, not from taste in general.

## Options and the pick

Do not offer the first idea in a field, the most famous mechanic, or the name made from the two most obvious words: the person already thought of those. Options differ in kind, not as small variations of one safe idea. One may be the safe one, but its `why` says so, and it is never the only real choice. Always recommend one option and say why; do not hide behind a neutral list.

## The escape becomes a default

An escape that worked once turns into the new safe pick if you use it again. Do not reach for these by reflex:

- An old, analog or printed look chosen because it reads as "not AI", not because the subject asks for it.
- The same small palette, the same paper, the same texture, the same wobbly line from one piece to the next.
- Made-up names that all come from one stock: sea and tide words, old trades, rope and salt, small English towns. Take names from the way the people who live there name things, not from a list of objects found in the place.
- A cream, beige or off-white ground. Take the ground from the subject: its night, its wall, its water, its screen.
- Exact times, dates and numbers used only to look concrete.
- A clever frame borrowed from another domain, such as a table, a map, a form or a schedule, laid over the subject.
- A small numbered label in the corner and a dry joke in the fine print.
- A small town, a named craftsman, a quiet magic rule and an odd object in the last line.

If two of your options share their main move, one of them is a default now. Pick a different kind of move.

## Check before you reply

For every option:

1. Could this be the answer to a different project? If yes, it is too general.
2. Is this what most people would make first? If yes, move it.
3. Can I say why this fits here, in one sentence, using a fact about this project? If not, the choice has no reason.

Write all text in the language of the brief, unless the project context names another language for that kind of text (a fixed language for project names, game names or a store page); those rules win. Keep the length and format the brief sets: a tagline stays a tagline.

Do not invent facts to make an option concrete. Use the real details from the brief and the context. If a made-up detail could pass for a real one and do harm (a price, a weight, a claim about the product), leave it out.
